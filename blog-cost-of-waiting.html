The Cost of Waiting: Why Delaying a Construction Project Can Cost More Than You Think

When homeowners consider a major renovation, addition, or new construction project, one of the most common instincts is to wait.

Wait until next year.
Wait until interest rates change.
Wait until material prices come down.
Wait until the market feels a little more predictable.

On the surface, waiting can feel financially responsible.

But construction does not stand still.

In Westchester County, New York City, and the surrounding metropolitan area, the price of building is constantly being affected by labor costs, material pricing, transportation, tariffs, subcontractor availability, permitting requirements, insurance, and overall market demand.

And historically, waiting for construction to become meaningfully cheaper has often proved to be an expensive strategy.

Construction Has Its Own Inflation Rate

One of the biggest misconceptions about construction is that project costs move at roughly the same rate as normal consumer inflation.

They don't.

Construction has its own economy.

According to Rider Levett Bucknall's Q2 2026 construction cost report, construction costs in the New York market were approximately 4.46% higher than a year earlier.

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reported an even broader increase: final-demand construction prices nationally were 5.2% higher in July 2026 than in July 2025.

Consider what that means for a substantial residential project.

A project priced around $500,000 today experiencing approximately 4.5% annual escalation represents more than $22,000 in additional cost after only one year.

On a $1 million project, the same escalation approaches $45,000.

And that assumes the project simply follows the overall construction market.

Certain materials can move considerably faster.

Look at Aluminum

Aluminum is a good example of why waiting for material prices to "settle down" can be dangerous.

Aluminum is found throughout modern residential construction: windows, doors, flashing, gutters, railings, exterior systems, mechanical components, roofing assemblies and architectural details.

According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the Producer Price Index for aluminum mill shapes was 40.5% higher in July 2026 than one year earlier.

A month earlier, the year-over-year increase had reached 52.4%.

Copper and brass mill shapes were also up 26% year over year in June, while steel mill products were approximately 16.9% higher.

Those increases eventually work their way downstream.

A homeowner may never purchase a sheet of aluminum or a coil of copper directly, but their window manufacturer does. Their roofer does. Their HVAC supplier does. Their electrician does. Their gutter fabricator does.

Eventually, those costs arrive at the project.

In Westchester and New York City, Labor Matters Just as Much

Construction in the New York metropolitan area is unusually labor-intensive.

The pool of experienced carpenters, masons, electricians, plumbers, roofers, tile setters, painters and finish craftsmen is finite.

The best trades are often booked months in advance.

As wages, insurance, workers' compensation, fuel and operating costs increase, labor pricing follows.

This is particularly important in Westchester, where many homes require more custom work than a standardized new-development project.

An older home in Bronxville may uncover framing that needs correction.

A Scarsdale renovation may involve complicated structural work and high-performance exterior assemblies.

A Rye or Larchmont home may require extensive waterproofing, custom millwork or specialty exterior detailing.

A Manhattan renovation introduces another layer of logistics, building rules, deliveries, vertical transportation, insurance and scheduling.

These projects depend heavily on skilled people.

And skilled labor rarely gets cheaper over time.

Waiting Doesn't Freeze Your Estimate

This is the point homeowners sometimes overlook.

A construction proposal is effectively a snapshot of the market at a particular moment.

It reflects today's lumber.
Today's steel.
Today's windows.
Today's concrete.
Today's subcontractor pricing.
Today's labor market.
Today's transportation costs.
Today's insurance environment.

If a project is placed on hold for twelve or eighteen months, the original number cannot simply be pulled back out of a drawer.

The project has to be priced again.

Sometimes the difference is modest.

Sometimes it isn't.

The $1 Million Renovation That Waits Two Years

Imagine a homeowner in Westchester planning a $1 million renovation.

Instead of starting, they decide to wait two years.

If construction costs increased at approximately 4.5% annually during that period, that same scope could approach roughly $1.09 million before considering design changes, permit changes, financing costs or unexpected increases in individual materials.

Approximately $90,000 hasn't purchased another bathroom.

It hasn't purchased better windows.

It hasn't upgraded the kitchen.

It hasn't created additional square footage.

It has simply purchased substantially the same project two years later.

That is the hidden cost of waiting.

Then There Is Scope Creep

Time has another effect on projects: plans tend to grow.

The longer a project sits in design, the more opportunities there are to reconsider decisions.

Maybe the kitchen becomes larger.

Then the windows change.

Then another bathroom is added.

Then the exterior cladding is reconsidered.

Then landscaping enters the project.

Thoughtful design is important, and owners should absolutely take enough time to make good decisions.

But there is a difference between planning carefully and remaining indefinitely undecided.

One creates value.

The other can create cost.

What If Material Prices Come Down?

They might.

Construction markets are cyclical, and individual commodities can absolutely fall.

Lumber has demonstrated this dramatically over the past several years. Metals rise and fall. Fuel fluctuates. Supply chains improve.

So starting immediately is not a guarantee that every individual material is at its lowest possible price.

But that is not really the decision homeowners are making.

They aren't buying aluminum futures.

They're building a house.

For the total cost of a project to meaningfully decline, reductions in certain commodities need to outweigh increases in labor, subcontractor pricing, insurance, transportation and dozens of other inputs.

In New York, that can be a difficult bet.

Rider Levett Bucknall reported New York construction cost growth of approximately 4.03% at the end of 2025, 4.40% in Q1 2026 and 4.46% by Q2 2026.

The trend is remarkably consistent.

The Better Strategy: Build When the Project Is Ready

None of this means a homeowner should rush into construction.

A rushed project can be considerably more expensive than a properly planned one.

Architectural drawings should be complete.

Structural questions should be resolved.

Major materials should be selected.

The contractor should understand the scope.

The budget should contain an appropriate contingency.

The team should know what it is building before demolition begins.

But once those pieces are in place, delaying simply in the hope that construction will be cheaper next year can create the opposite result.

There is enormous value in making decisions early.

A good builder can also use early planning to lock in subcontractors, identify long-lead materials, place orders strategically and reduce exposure to future price increases.

Time Is a Line Item, Even When You Can't See It

Construction budgets show lumber, masonry, roofing, electrical, plumbing, finishes and labor.

They rarely contain a line labeled "waiting."

But perhaps they should.

Every month between deciding to build and actually building exposes a project to a changing market.

Sometimes that movement works in your favor.

More often, especially over longer periods, rising labor and construction costs quietly move the finish line farther away.

For homeowners considering a renovation, addition or new home in Westchester County or New York City, the question should therefore not simply be:

"Should we wait until construction gets cheaper?"

A better question is:

"What will it cost us if it doesn't?"

At Prufrock Builders, we believe the best protection against construction inflation isn't rushing. It's planning thoroughly, pricing intelligently and moving decisively once the project is ready.

Because while you can take your time designing the right home, the market won't necessarily wait with you.
